SLIDE 17 — BASELINE COMBINED MODELS

We first reproduced two ways of combining segmentation and classification.
The sequential dual-stage approach uses DeepLabV3+ to predict a lesion mask,
converts it into an ROI, and uses EfficientNet-B0 to classify the cropped lesion.

MTL-OCA instead uses one shared encoder with separate segmentation and
classification heads. This gave us a choice between a shared multi-task model
and a modular pipeline of task-specific specialists.

[Transition]
We therefore first asked: which of these two directions should we improve?


SLIDE 18 — WHY WE CHOSE THE DUAL-STAGE PIPELINE

We chose dual-stage for four reasons. It is modular, so both stages can be
improved independently. It also supports controlled experiments: we can keep
Stage 2 unchanged, replace only Stage 1, and directly measure the effect of
better segmentation.

It also reuses our strongest task-specific models and produces an explicit mask
and ROI, making the decision more interpretable than a class label alone.

For these reasons, we extended and rigorously evaluated the dual-stage pipeline.


SLIDE 19 — IMPROVED SEGMENTER

Our first improvement targets Stage 1.

Transfer learning was especially effective in our segmentation baselines, so we
built a U-Net with an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B4 encoder. We also tested
Focal Tversky plus BCE loss to emphasise false negatives.

At inference, TTA averages predictions from original, flipped, and multi-scale
images. Post-processing then fills small gaps and removes isolated false-positive
regions. We test each component separately instead of assuming it helps.


SLIDE 20 — SEGMENTER ABLATION

[Point to the table and read down the rows.]

The pretrained encoder is the decisive component. It increases Dice from 0.648
to 0.765, a gain of 0.117, while reducing variability across test images.

Focal Tversky does not improve overall Dice, but it increases pixel recall from
0.763 to 0.800. Therefore, it mainly changes the precision-recall trade-off.

TTA and post-processing produce no measurable Dice improvement. On small and
noisy BUSI data, transfer learning contributes far more than additional loss or
inference complexity.


SLIDE 21 — IMPROVED DUAL-STAGE PIPELINE

We then placed the improved segmenter into the dual-stage pipeline. We select the
largest connected component of its mask, calculate a bounding box, and add a
fifteen-percent margin to preserve surrounding tissue. This ROI is passed to the
Stage-2 EfficientNet-B0 classifier.

We first performed a controlled substitution: only Stage 1 was replaced, while the
ROI coupling and Stage 2 were kept unchanged. This isolated the contribution of
segmentation quality.

We then strengthened Stage 2 by training on both ground-truth and predicted-mask
ROIs, and added OOF masks, five-seed ensembling, and bootstrap confidence
intervals.


SLIDE 22 — OOF AND DUAL-STAGE ABLATION

Stage 2 must learn from realistic predicted ROIs. If a segmenter has already seen
an image, its mask can be unrealistically clean.

With five-fold cross-fitting, each fold receives masks from a segmenter trained
only on the other four folds. Every training image therefore receives a mask from
a model that never saw it, reducing the clean-training versus imperfect-testing
mismatch.

[Point to the three rows.]

We keep the OOF pipeline fixed and change only the Stage-2 input: ROI image alone,
ROI plus six shape features, or ROI plus radiomics features.

Although the hand-crafted features slightly increase the average single-seed F1,
they do not improve the final ensemble. Pure CNN and shape features both achieve
0.875 with the same confidence interval, while radiomics decreases to 0.863.

The hand-crafted features therefore provide no reliable incremental benefit, so
we retain the simpler pure-CNN configuration.


SLIDE 25 — FINAL DUAL-STAGE INTERPRETATION

The dual-stage experiments give us two main conclusions.

First, improving Stage-1 Dice from 0.739 to 0.760 changes the initial pipeline F1
by only 0.003. The original mask was already sufficient for ROI localisation, so
the initial bottleneck was the classifier, not the exact mask boundary.

Second, the apparent shape and radiomics gains disappear under multi-seed
evaluation and confidence intervals. Ensembling raises the pure-CNN per-seed mean
of 0.847 to 0.875, with a 95-percent confidence interval from 0.80 to 0.94.

Our contribution is therefore a controlled, leakage-aware pipeline that identifies
which improvements are actually reliable.


SHORT Q&A ANSWERS

1. What is TTA?
TTA means test-time augmentation. We predict the original, flipped, and multi-scale
versions of the same image, transform the outputs back, and average their probability
maps. In our ablation, it did not provide a measurable Dice improvement.

2. What is the largest connected component?
It is the largest continuous foreground region in the predicted binary mask. We
assume this main region is the lesion and use it to calculate the ROI bounding box,
while small isolated regions are treated as likely false positives.

3. Why do we need OOF masks?
OOF prevents the Stage-2 training masks from being generated by a segmenter that has
already seen the same images. It produces more realistic predicted-mask crops and
reduces in-fold optimism.

4. Why is pure CNN selected instead of shape or radiomics?
Pure CNN and shape features have the same final ensemble F1 of 0.875 and identical
confidence intervals. Radiomics is lower at 0.863. Since the hand-crafted features
provide no reliable improvement, we select the simpler pure-CNN configuration.

5. Are hand-crafted features useless?
Not in general. Our conclusion is limited to this dataset and evaluation protocol:
they provide no reliable additional benefit over the pure-CNN baseline on BUSI.
